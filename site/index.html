One 8 GB card can't run a 70B model. Two hundred of them can.

DLNE is a peer-to-peer inference engine. It pools VRAM, system RAM and CPU threads across a swarm of ordinary machines and splits the tensor load between them — so the model you run is limited by the network, not by the card in your desk.

The mission

Frontier models are gated by two walls. Neither one is a law of physics.

Wall 01 — hardware

The model doesn't fit

A 70B model in reasonable quantisation wants more VRAM than any consumer card ships with. The weights aren't the problem — the fact that they all have to sit behind one PCIe slot is. Meanwhile the GPU in the machine next to yours is idle, and so is the one after that.

Wall 02 — access

So you rent it instead

The alternative is metered access to someone else's hardware, where every prompt is billed, logged, rate-limited and subject to a terms-of-service change you'll read about afterwards. Your conversation becomes their training corpus, and the model can be deprecated out from under you.

DLNE removes both walls with the same move: treat the community's idle hardware as one machine. Contribute compute, draw on the pool — no invoice, no API key, no third party in the middle of your prompt.

How the swarm runs a model

Layers go out to the nodes. Tokens come back.

Nothing is scheduled by hand. The Coordinator knows the swarm's live hardware, sizes the context to what actually fits, and places the split itself.

01  /  aggregate

The Coordinator counts the pool

Every node heartbeats its usable VRAM, RAM and cores over a signed channel. The hub sums them into one budget and reads each model's real memory cost straight from its GGUF header rather than guessing at it.

02  /  split

The model is cut across nodes

Tensor layers are distributed over the workers that can hold them, then executed in sequence through the encrypted tunnel. Speculative decoding pairs a heavyweight model with a small draft model to push whole blocks of tokens at once.

03  /  yield

Your machine stays yours

Smart Yielding watches local load. Launch a game or a render and the node steps out of the rotation; the swarm redistributes the work and picks you back up when you're done.

Pick your role

Four ways to be part of the network

You choose this once, during setup. The value in the box is what actually lands in your node's config — nothing here is hidden from you.

coordinator_only

Coordinator — the hub

Hosts the WebUI, holds the tunnel, aggregates the swarm's hardware and balances the load. Runs the network without spending its own GPU on inference.

Best for: a machine with steady uptime and a decent connection.

worker_only

Worker — the muscle

Contributes GPU and CPU compute to the pool and nothing else. Sits quietly in the background, yields the moment you need the machine back.

Best for: gaming rigs, spare desktops, anything idle overnight.

coordinator_hybrid

Hybrid — hub and muscle

Runs the Coordinator and contributes its own hardware to the tensor split at the same time. One box, both jobs.

Best for: a strong workstation you're happy to leave on.

redundancy_worker

Redundancy — the failsafe

Works like a standard worker but keeps models and state synced with the primary. If the hub drops, a time-offset election promotes a standby on cryptographically signed signals — no split brain, no lost swarm.

Best for: always-on servers keeping the network alive.

What you get

Built for people who'd rather own the stack

Your prompts stay in the tunnel

Nodes talk to each other over WireGuard. Prompts and model shards are encrypted before they leave your machine and never touch a public cloud endpoint.

Chat history the hub can't read

Conversations are encrypted with AES-GCM and stored in your own browser. The Coordinator routes the tensors; it keeps no permanent record of what you said.

Drop-in for Ollama

The hub speaks the Ollama API on :11434. Point AnythingLLM, SillyTavern or OpenWebUI at it and they use the whole swarm without a single change.

Speculative decoding

A lightweight draft model proposes token blocks that the full model verifies in one pass, so generation isn't paced by a single node's round trip.

Contribution clause

Seed compute, pull compute. The hardware you lend the swarm is what buys you access to everyone else's — no subscription, no credits.

Signed updates, always asked

Releases are signed and hash-verified before anything is swapped in, and a node never updates itself behind your back — you get the notes and a choice.

Hardware

If it has run a game in the last decade, it can run a shard

Builds are split by backend so old cards aren't held back by a compiler target they can't use. Setup detects your hardware and picks for you — the table is here so you can overrule it.

Build Backend Covers
Windows · CUDA latest NVIDIA CUDA Ampere, Ada Lovelace, Hopper — RTX 3000/4000/5000, H100. Needs current drivers.
Windows · CUDA 11.8 NVIDIA CUDA Maxwell, Pascal, Volta, Turing — GTX 900/1000/1600, RTX 2000, Titan V. The compatibility tier.
Windows · Vulkan Vulkan AMD Radeon, Intel Arc, modern integrated graphics — and NVIDIA cards on stubborn drivers.
Linux · Vulkan Vulkan AMD and Intel on Mesa, NVIDIA on the proprietary driver. Compute cards and mining rigs included.
macOS · Apple Silicon Metal M-series Macs, using unified memory as the pool.

No GPU? You can still join. The swarm spills into system RAM when VRAM runs out. Because a slow node holds up everyone behind it, a CPU-only node has to commit at least 8 GB of RAM and 2 physical cores before it is admitted. Multi-GPU rigs go the other way: allocate a precise VRAM ceiling per card, or hand over everything.

How to join

One file, one installer, about five minutes

The swarm is invite-gated during alpha, so the sequence starts on Discord. Your private key is generated on your machine and never leaves it — not even to the Coordinator.

  1. Join the Discord

    Say hello in the intro channel and tell us what you're running — GPU, VRAM, and roughly when the machine is idle. That's what tells us which build to point you at.

  2. Collect your join file

    You'll be handed one small .dlnejoin file. It carries the Coordinator's address and the network key — enough to prove you belong here, and deliberately no private key of any kind.

    # network_join.dlnejoin DLNE_JOIN_VERSION=1 COORDINATOR_HOST=<hub address> COORDINATOR_PORT=11435 NETWORK_KEY=<cluster secret> WG_ENDPOINT=<hub>:51820

  3. Download the build for your hardware

    Grab the signed release for your platform, or run the one-shot installer and let it detect the card, pull the right variant and verify the download against a published hash before it unpacks anything.

  4. Drop the file in and run setup

    Put the join file next to the app and start it. The wizard walks you through role, contribution limits and firewall rules — and along the way your node generates its own WireGuard keypair, sends only the public half to the Coordinator, and gets its address on the tunnel back.

  5. You're in the swarm

    The node benchmarks itself, registers its hardware and starts taking work. Open the WebUI to chat against the pooled network, or point your existing frontend at the Coordinator and carry on as normal.

Questions

The things everyone asks first

Is it free?

Yes. The network is the community's hardware, so there's nothing to bill for. Contribute something — even CPU only — and you can draw on the pool.

Will it slow my machine down?

It shouldn't. Smart Yielding detects heavy local use and hands your share of the work to other nodes, so a game or a render takes the machine back immediately rather than fighting inference for the GPU.

Do I need to forward ports?

No. Nodes meet inside a WireGuard tunnel that traverses NAT, so your internal addresses are never exposed and your router doesn't need touching.

Can the Coordinator read my chats?

No. History is encrypted with AES-GCM in your browser and stays there. The hub processes tensors in flight and keeps no permanent copy of your conversations.

Does my private key ever leave my machine?

Never. Your node generates its own WireGuard keypair locally and sends only the public half when it enrols. That's why one join file can be handed to the whole network without weakening anyone on it.

What state is the project actually in?

Alpha, and honestly so. The engine, the tunnel, failover and the WebUI all work; what's being hardened now is the long tail of real hardware and real home networks. Expect rough edges, and expect them to get fixed quickly.
